# Keep your voice, swap your face: a build plan

For a code-rendered studio, the best way to make faceless videos in a creator's real voice is to skip the photoreal avatar by default. Record or clone the voice, drive scene timing from word timestamps, and put a stylized mascot or a small picture-in-picture avatar in a reserved zone of the existing renderer. The voice is the easy part: recording it yourself is the highest-fidelity and lowest-risk option, and a hosted clone (ElevenLabs, Cartesia) costs about $5-22 per month for scripted narration. A lip-synced face is the harder part. It is a commodity if you rent it (HeyGen from $29/month, Hedra or fal.ai by the second) and a GPU project if you self-host. The binding constraint is rules, not technology. YouTube's July 2026 "inauthentic content" clarification demonetizes templated, mass-produced AI output and AI "personas" giving finance, health or legal advice, while the EU AI Act's deepfake disclosure duty has applied since 2 August 2026. Controlled evidence says a realistic avatar can match a real presenter on trust and retention, but only one study, on presenter videos rather than short clips, supports this. Budgets run from about $0 to about $200 per month. Most figures below are vendor or aggregator numbers that conflict across sources, so verify them on vendor pages before buying.

## Faceless still works, but only with bespoke visuals and a real angle

Sources broadly agree that faceless channels still work in 2026 but the easy era is over: low-effort, mass-produced content is what stopped working, while explainers, history and facts, true crime, motivational and finance niches persist ([Yoola](https://yoola.com/blog/best-faceless-youtube-niches-that-still-work-in-2026), [Pictory](https://pictory.ai/blog/faceless-youtube-channel-ai)). Almost all of this evidence comes from tool vendors, and no source gave independent retention or click-through data comparing avatar, cartoon, kinetic-type and stock formats. The format ranking that follows is inference. Third-party listings show the pattern of successful anonymous channels: Kurzgesagt (flat-design animation plus researched scripts, loyalty from editorial identity rather than a host) and Tasty (anonymous overhead recipe video) ([GoFaceless](https://www.gofaceless.ai/blog/faceless-youtube-channel-examples), [VidPros](https://vidpros.com/top-faceless-youtube-channels)). Subscriber and revenue figures in those listings are unverified.

The studio's strength lines up with this. Motion-graphics explainers and kinetic typography over a voiceover make the visual bespoke to each script, which is the property YouTube's policy rewards and generic stock-plus-text-to-speech lacks. An avatar is optional for presence and trust, not required. On audience trust, the best controlled evidence is a University of South Florida study (n=290) comparing a real presenter with a hyper-realistic avatar of the same person: there was no significant difference in retention, engagement or trust, and the only significant result (p=.003) was lower retention when viewers were told it was an avatar ([USF thesis](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/etd/10088/)). TechSmith's instructional-video findings add a design rule: a small picture-in-picture avatar gave the best learning retention, while full-screen avatars made robotic traits easier to spot and lowered quality ratings ([TechSmith](https://techsmith.com/blog/ai-voices-avatars-in-training-videos)). That matches the studio's own zone rule, where a window holds the speaker and nothing covers the face.

## The voice layer: record first, clone second, check the language

Recording remains the gold standard. A dynamic USB or XLR microphone in a soft-furnished, quiet room beats any fix-it-later processing; roundups name the Samson Q2U (about $69), Rode PodMic USB (about $99) and Shure MV7+ (about $279) as budget, mid and premium picks ([Zac Johnson roundup](https://zacjohnson.com/?p=939)). AI cleanup (Adobe Enhance Speech, Descript Studio Sound, Resolve Voice Isolation) rescues bad audio but can leave the voice sounding slightly re-recorded ([Podcast Haven](https://thepodcasthaven.com/adobe-speech-enhancement-vs-descript-studio-sound-a-shootout/)). If the recording will also train a clone, keep it dry and lightly processed; that advice is my inference, not sourced.

When typing a script is faster than recording, a hosted clone works. ElevenLabs lists Starter at $6 with Instant Voice Clone, Creator at $22 with one Professional Voice Clone, and Pro at $99 ([ElevenLabs pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing)), though the page is internally inconsistent on Starter and Creator prices. Professional cloning needs Creator or above and a voice-captcha check that the samples are the account holder's own voice ([ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/capabilities/voices)). Cartesia Pro is $5 per month with a commercial licence and instant cloning ([Cartesia pricing](https://cartesia.ai/pricing)). Language matters: Eleven v3 lists 74 languages including Arabic, Hindi and Urdu, while Multilingual v2 lacks Urdu ([ElevenLabs language help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/other/what-languages-do-you-support)). No independent Urdu quality metric was found, and no open-source model with verified zero-shot Urdu cloning surfaced, so an Urdu-speaking creator should test a sample before subscribing.

Free local cloning exists but carries licence traps. Chatterbox (MIT, 10-second reference, Hindi in the multilingual model, Perth watermark on every output) and Kokoro (Apache 2.0, preset voices only, no cloning of your own voice) are clean; F5-TTS weights are CC-BY-NC, XTTS-v2 uses the Coqui Public Model License, and IndexTTS and Fish Speech weights use custom licences ([Chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox), [F5-TTS](https://github.com/SWivid/F5-TTS), [Coqui XTTS](https://coqui-tts.readthedocs.io/en/latest/models/xtts.html)). A consumer NVIDIA GPU of roughly 8-12 GB should run most of them, but that figure is my estimate; no source documents it.

## Three ways to give the voice a face, from cheapest to heaviest

The cheapest route needs no generated human at all. Rhubarb Lip Sync takes an audio file and outputs mouth-shape cues (six basic shapes plus optional extras) as TSV, XML or JSON ([Rhubarb README](https://github.com/DanielSWolf/rhubarb-lip-sync)). Applying those cues per frame to an SVG or canvas mascot fits the studio's `window.seek(t)` contract exactly: deterministic, seekable, no identity risk, near-zero compute. The catch is that Rhubarb's English recognizer is PocketSphinx and the language-independent phonetic mode is less precise, which matters for non-English voices, and its licence was not confirmed from the primary repo. A hand-built or commissioned rig is a one-time cost (custom 2D VTuber models run roughly $200-$1,000 on commission, per a marketing-grade source), and the live-capture VTuber tools are awkward to render offline.

The middle route rents a photo-driven animator. Hedra animates one image, photoreal or cartoon, to supplied audio, requires image plus audio, and bills by the second; its API lists lip-sync from 2.5 cents per second with free voice cloning ([Hedra docs](https://hedra.com/docs/pages/developer/guides/generate-avatar-video), [Hedra pricing](https://www.hedra.com/pricing)). Its consumer plans are in conflict across trackers (about $15-$150 before a reported September 2026 repricing), and 1080p was not confirmed. Kling Avatar and Captions' Mirage model also take image plus audio ([Scenario](https://www.scenario.com/models/kling-ai-avatar-2-pro), [Mirage API](https://help.mirage.app/docs/api/pricing)). Because they accept any WAV, any recorded or cloned voice works. Lip sync on stylized faces is reported as one of the weakest spots in AI video ([Heather Cooper](https://heatherbcooper.substack.com/p/ai-video-tools-are-evolving)), so expect to review every take.

The realistic route uses HeyGen or Synthesia. HeyGen's official page lists Creator at $29 per month (600 credits, 30-minute cap, 1080p), Pro at $49 (4K) and Business at $149 plus $20 per seat, with a free tier capped at 1 minute with a watermark ([HeyGen pricing](https://www.heygen.com/pricing)). Synthesia lists Starter at $29 monthly and Pro at $89 ([Synthesia pricing](https://www.synthesia.io/pricing)). Reviewers note repetitive facial patterns in videos over 10 minutes and uncanny emotional close-ups ([VMEG](https://www.vmeg.ai/blog/heygen-reviews/)), and credits drain fast: a third-party figure puts Avatar IV and V near 20 credits per minute against about 3 for Avatar III ([Arcade](https://www.arcade.software/post/heygen-pricing)). Stock presenters are shared across many users, so a "faceless" channel built on them has no identity; a custom avatar of yourself defeats the purpose.

Self-hosting is the heavy option. Lip-sync-only models (MuseTalk, MIT; LatentSync, Apache 2.0) run on 4-18 GB cards but only animate the mouth region ([MuseTalk](https://github.com/TMElyralab/MuseTalk), [LatentSync](https://github.com/bytedance/LatentSync)). Whole-video diffusion avatars give real head motion from one image but need far more. InfiniteTalk (Apache 2.0) stays good to about a minute at 480p or 720p ([InfiniteTalk](https://github.com/MeiGen-AI/InfiniteTalk)); StableAvatar (MIT) needs about 18 GB at full load and renders 5 seconds in about 3 minutes on an RTX 4090 ([StableAvatar](https://github.com/Francis-Rings/StableAvatar)); Wan2.2-S2V's official single-GPU command needs 80 GB ([Wan2.2-S2V](https://huggingface.co/Wan-AI/Wan2.2-S2V-14B)). My arithmetic puts a 24 GB card at 20-40 times slower than real time. Wav2Lip is non-commercial and should be excluded from client work ([Wav2Lip](https://github.com/Rudrabha/Wav2Lip)). Rental changes the math: RunPod lists a 4090 at $0.34-$0.89 per hour and an A100 80 GB at $1.19-$1.79 ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/gpu-cloud/pricing)), while fal.ai charges $0.20 per output second for InfiniteTalk, about $12 per video minute at 480p ([fal InfiniteTalk](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/infinitalk)). Self-hosting a working pipeline on a rented GPU costs cents to a few dollars per video minute by my estimate, versus $8-$12 on hosted APIs, but no source measured a rental render cost. Pre-rendered clips of 15-60 seconds slot into the studio as video layers, so keep avatar beats short and composite them.

## Compositing the avatar into the code-rendered pipeline

The notes identify six approaches, all derived from documentation rather than tested in this environment. First, voiceover-first: transcribe the final audio with word timestamps and feed them into `window.seek(t)` so each animation pictures the words being said, then mux the audio after render. Second, picture-in-picture: render the graphics to `silent.mp4`, then overlay the avatar clip in a reserved window with ffmpeg, forcing the avatar to the same fps as the render to avoid drift and using its audio, or the original voice, as the master track. Third, transparent overlay: HeyGen's API returns alpha WebM when `output_format` is `webm`, requires a matting-trained avatar, and is unavailable for Cinematic Avatar ([HeyGen developer docs](https://developers.heygen.com/transparent-background-videos)). ffmpeg pitfalls are real: VP9 alpha needs `-c:v libvpx-vp9` forced before `-i` or the default decoder may drop the alpha, and `ffprobe` can report yuv420p even when alpha exists ([ffmpeg trac #8344](https://fftrac-bg.ffmpeg.org/ticket/8344)). Converting to ProRes 4444 or a PNG sequence is a suggested workaround that nobody has tested here.

Fourth, green screen with `chromakey` plus `despill` works when alpha is unavailable but leaves fringes. Fifth, an in-page `<video>` seeked per frame would keep everything inside `render.mjs`, but per-frame seek accuracy is a known risk and needs `window.ready` plus a test render. Sixth, a mascot driven by Rhubarb cues avoids video decoding entirely and is the lowest-risk path for determinism. Hard cuts must still be listed in `window.hardCuts`, and the existing `qa.mjs` gate applies. Synthesia's native alpha export was not confirmed; third-party background removers exist ([VideoBGRemover](https://videobgremover.com/ai-video/synthesia)).

## Rules: disclosure, monetization and consent decide how realistic you can go

On YouTube, cloning your own voice for voiceovers is explicitly exempt from the altered-or-synthetic disclosure, as are AI-assisted scripts and fully animated or non-realistic content; disclosure is required when a realistic person appears to say or do something they did not ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). The page is silent on AI presenter avatars, so a photoreal avatar of yourself is a grey area where a label costs little: disclosure alone does not limit audience or monetization. Monetization is the larger risk. "Inauthentic content" (renamed from "repetitious content" on 15 July 2025) bars slideshows, templated storylines and mass-produced AI output, yet allows AI used to visualize a character or story you invented ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392)). The July 2026 clarification adds AI-generated personas on sensitive topics such as finance, legal and health as a third category, and enforcement is channel-level ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/)). Finance is reportedly the highest-paying faceless niche and also the one an AI host cannot monetize, so a finance channel needs a stylized mascot or no host plus the creator's own voice and editorial judgment. Reports of mass terminations and a February 2027 partner-programme overhaul rest on single secondary sources and should not drive decisions.

Elsewhere the picture is thinner. Meta may require an "AI info" label for photorealistic video or realistic audio ([Meta Help Center](https://www.meta.com/en-gb/help/artificial-intelligence/1783222608822690/)) and penalizes repeated unoriginal content, not AI itself ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=187296)). TikTok has a creator-applied AI-generated label and requires clear labeling of realistic synthetic media ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/new-labels-for-disclosing-ai-generated-content?lang=en)), but whether the label is mandatory or encouraged differs between secondary blogs, and sources conflict on whether fully AI content earns Creator Rewards. No primary TikTok or Meta policy pages were read, and nothing was found for X.

Law adds a floor. EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duties applied from 2 August 2026; deployers must visibly disclose deepfakes, with penalties up to EUR 15m or 3% of turnover ([ICTRecht](https://www.ictrecht.nl/en/blog/heads-up-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-effect)). Whether a first-person synthetic presenter of yourself counts as a deepfake is unsettled in the sources found. India's IT Rules amendments, effective 20 February 2026, require platforms to label synthetic audio and visual content ([S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/articles/india-tightens-oversight-on-ai-generated-content-under-it-rules/)). New York requires ad disclosure of a fully simulated human from 9 June 2026 ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai)), and the FTC bans AI-generated fake testimonials ([Sidley](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2024/08/30/u-s-ftcs-new-rule-on-fake-and-ai-generated-reviews-and-social-media-bots/)). The federal NO FAKES Act stalled in October 2026 ([Daily Journal](https://www.dailyjournal.com/article/395055-no-fakes-act-dead-for-now-as-cruz-objects-to-unanimous-consent-request)). Vendor terms enforce consent: ElevenLabs requires your own or authorized voice and allows commercial use only on paid plans ([ElevenLabs Terms](https://elevenlabs.io/terms-of-use)), HeyGen's free-plan outputs are non-commercial ([HeyGen Terms](https://www.heygen.com/terms)), and Synthesia requires a second consent video for personal avatars ([Synthesia governance](https://www.synthesia.io/ai-governance)). This is a reading of secondary sources, not legal advice.

## Budget tiers and the resource checklist

The tier figures are planning estimates built from conflicting vendor prices, not quotes. One vendor workflow estimate puts a SaaS faceless-reel stack at roughly $35-$110 per month and about 20 minutes per clip after about 2 hours of setup ([ToolJunction](https://www.tooljunction.io/workflows/ai-shortform-video-reels-workflow)); one creator reported about 2 hours for a single long-form video ([crepal](https://crepal.ai/blog/aivideo/blog-script-to-video-youtube-automation/)). Studio-rendered work takes longer per piece than a template tool because every scene is code, so the time estimates in the table are mine.

| Resource | Free (about $0) | About $50 per month | About $200 per month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice | Own recording, or Chatterbox/Kokoro local | ElevenLabs Creator ($22) or Cartesia Pro ($5) | ElevenLabs Pro ($99), Professional Voice Clone |
| Face | Rhubarb-driven mascot in the studio; free-tier tools with watermark | Hedra or Kling by the second, or HeyGen Creator ($29), one of these | HeyGen Pro/Business ($49-$149) or rented-GPU InfiniteTalk |
| Captions and edit | Whisper word timestamps, studio-rendered captions, ffmpeg, DaVinci Resolve | CapCut Pro or Submagic Starter ($19) if wanted | Descript ($24), extra AI clip credits |
| Hardware | PC that runs Chromium and ffmpeg; USB mic $0-$100 | Same | Same, plus rented 4090/A100 at $0.34-$1.79 per hour as needed |
| Time per 30 s reel | about 1-3 h at first, falling with templates | about 30-60 min | about 20-40 min |
| Time per 10 min video | about 8-20 h | about 5-10 h | about 3-6 h |

Hardware beyond that depends on the self-hosting choice. An 8 GB GPU with 16 GB RAM covers MuseTalk or LatentSync 1.5; a 24 GB card with 64 GB RAM and a few hundred gigabytes of NVMe covers InfiniteTalk fp8 or StableAvatar at 480p, slowly; anything for 720p long-form means renting 48-80 GB. These tiers are inferences, since no model publishes minimum PC specs and the disk and RAM sizes are my estimates. The RAM and offload relationship is supported only by an indirect ComfyUI guide about a different model ([InstaSD](https://www.instasd.com/post/comfyui-vram-offloading-guide)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "How do I replace my face with an avatar" is the wrong frame for this studio. The cheaper, safer and more on-brand answer is to move the human into the voice and the editorial judgment, and let code-rendered motion carry the picture. A photoreal talking head adds cost, uncanny risk, disclosure duties and, on sensitive topics, monetization risk, in exchange for presence that a mascot or a small picture-in-picture window can supply. The evidence for avatars matching real faces is a single study of presenter videos, so it should not be stretched to Reels or Shorts.

Three things should be settled before committing money. First, run a test of the creator's own language with ElevenLabs and one image-to-video tool (Hedra or Kling) on the same 20-second script, since Urdu support and stylized lip-sync quality are both vendor claims with no independent data. Second, run one alpha-overlay and one per-frame `<video>` seek test in `render.mjs`, because neither is verified. Third, pick one niche and an original structure per video: the policy-level threat to this plan is looking templated, not looking synthetic.
